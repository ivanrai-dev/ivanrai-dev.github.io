AI adoption · Non-engineering roles

Your job already contains the instructions. You just never wrote them down.

This is about agentic workflows — what they actually are, why they are not chatbots, and how to tell whether the work sitting on your desk today could be handed over. There is nothing to install and nothing to sign up for. There are a few things to click.

About 15 minutes if you do the exercises. About 4 if you skim.

01 — Clearing the ground

What an agentic workflow is not

It is not a chatbot. A chatbot waits to be spoken to, answers from what it already knows, and then forgets you existed. It has no access to anything real and produces nothing that lands anywhere.

An agentic workflow is triggered by something, reads real sources, follows a procedure someone wrote down, produces actual work, and files that work where it belongs. The difference is not intelligence. It is access, instructions, and a place for the output to go.

An LLM chatbot
ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity. Underneath, these are systems that measure closeness and similarity mathematically to produce the most plausible next answer. They hold no knowledge beyond what they were trained on: you type, and they answer from general knowledge. They cannot see your accounts, your inbox, or last quarter's numbers. Useful for phrasing an email. Useless for doing your job.
Agentic coding
The same underlying model, given real access to a codebase and the ability to act on it. It reads the existing code, understands how it is structured, reviews it, suggests changes and writes new code that matches the conventions already there. The difference is not that it is cleverer. It is that it can see the actual work and act on it.
Agentic workflows
The same idea pointed at everything that is not code. Real access to the systems your work actually lives in, a written procedure for one specific task, and a person approving the result before anything happens. This is what the rest of this document is about.
02 — The idea in one sentence

It is a capable new colleague you hand a written procedure to

Picture the best new starter you have ever had. Quick, willing, tireless, never bored by the fiftieth repetition of the same task.

They also know nothing about how we do things. Not because they are slow — because nobody has told them yet. Everything they produce depends entirely on the quality of what you wrote down for them.

Which means the interesting question was never "how clever is the AI?" It is "how good are my instructions?"

You are about to be that new colleague. You start today, and your first shift is in a burger shop.

03 — The actual constraint

The reason this has not already happened

It is tempting to assume the thing holding organisations back is the technology. That the models are not good enough yet, or the tools are not ready, or nobody has found the budget.

That is not what is happening. The constraint is much less interesting than that, and much harder to fix with money.

Almost nothing an organisation does is written down. Not the org chart or the policy documents or the onboarding deck — those exist. The actual procedures. How this specific decision gets made, by whom, using what, and what makes it a yes rather than a no. That lives in people's heads, and the people holding the most of it are the busiest people in the building.

Why engineering went first
It has very little to do with engineers being more technical. Their work is already structured: the process is defined, the format is defined, the procedure is written down — that is what code is — and the knowledge base is easy to connect and share. When an agentic tool arrives, the instructions are already there waiting for it. Everywhere else in the company, they are not.
And there is a bill attached to that
If the procedure only exists in somebody's head, it leaves when they do. Not the documents and not the records — those stay. The thing that walks out is how the work was actually done: the sequence, the exceptions, the reasons behind the reasons, the things that were obvious to one person and invisible to everyone else. Every organisation has processes that nobody alive can fully explain, and every one of those started as somebody's undocumented competence. Writing procedures down is worth doing whether or not anything ever gets automated. Automation is simply the first reason most people find compelling enough to actually do it.

The question was never whether the AI is clever enough to do your job. It is whether anyone has ever written down how your job is done.

Your first shift

You have just started at a burger chain. There is an order in front of you for two burgers.

Two rounds. The first uses the kind of instructions most of us actually leave behind for other people. The second uses instructions written properly. The difference between those two rounds is the entire point of this document.

Round 1 of 2 — burger one
Note left by the last person on shift
Add sauce.

The bun, patty and cheese are done. Sauce is yours. Pick one and the burger goes out.

The order was ketchup and mayo, no mustard.

You had no way of knowing that.

You did nothing wrong — the instruction did. This is exactly what happens when a task is handed to someone without the detail they actually needed to do it. The work still gets done. It is just the wrong work, confidently.

Round 2 of 2 — burger two
The written procedure
Add ketchup, then add mayo, skip mustard. Then close the bun.

Same ingredients. Same you. Follow it in order.

Correct, first time.

Same colleague. Same ingredients. Same ability. The only thing that changed is what you wrote down.

That written procedure is the reusable part. Not the AI — the instruction. Write it once and anyone, or anything, can execute it the same way every time.

  • Order matters. Sauce after the bun closes is not the same burger.
  • Ambiguity breaks execution. "Add sauce" was not a small gap. It was the whole decision.
  • What you skip must be stated as explicitly as what you include. "Skip mustard" is an instruction, not an omission.

The anatomy of a workflow

Every automation worth building has the same six parts. Once you can see them, you can see them everywhere — including in the burger you just made.

SKILL — The part a person writes

What one actually looks like

Every part of the framework above is configuration except one. The skill is the part a person writes, and it is the only part that carries your judgement. Here is a real one, short enough to read in a minute.

SKILL: Pre-call account brief

TRIGGER
  Run when a client call is booked, the morning before the call.

GROUNDING/CONTEXT
  Read, for this account only:
    - the account record: contract, renewal date, open issues
    - the transcript of the most recent call
    - the email thread with the client, last 90 days
    - last quarter's performance numbers
  Do not use: personal notes, general web search, anything
  older than 12 months unless nothing newer exists.

PROCEDURE
  1. State the renewal date and how many weeks away it is.
  2. List open escalations. For each: what it is, who owns it,
     how long it has been open.
  3. Give the quarter-on-quarter movement in the headline
     number, and name the single largest driver of that
     movement. Do not report the number without the driver.
  4. List every commitment we made on the last call and mark
     each one done or outstanding.
  5. Flag anything the client raised twice. Repetition means
     it was not resolved the first time.

RULES
  - If a source is missing or empty, say so explicitly.
    Never fill a gap with a general statement.
  - Name the current primary contact from the account record,
    never from older material.
  - Six bullets maximum. This is read in a corridor.

HUMAN GATE
  Output goes to the account owner as a draft. Nothing is sent
  to the client and nothing is written to the record until
  they approve it.

WRITE-BACK
  On approval, save to the account record as a call note,
  dated, attached to the meeting.

That is the whole thing. Notice what is in it and what is not.

There is no cleverness in it and no attempt to sound like anything. It is the kind of note you would leave for a colleague covering your accounts while you are on holiday — except this one gets followed exactly, every time, without the colleague quietly filling in the gaps from experience you never wrote down.

The lines that look like overkill

Do not report the number without the driver — because somebody once did.

Never fill a gap with a general statement — because the first version did exactly that, fluently enough that nobody noticed for two weeks.

Six bullets maximum — because the first version was four paragraphs and nobody read it.

This is what early rounds of iteration actually produce. Not a better model — a longer list of things you did not know you knew.

04 — Going deeper on your own task

The decision underneath the task

Every task hides a decision. Every decision has three parts: the choice being made, who has the authority to make it, and the information needed to make it well. Most work that feels un-automatable feels that way because the choice is invisible — it has collapsed into habit, and the gathering around it has quietly expanded to fill the day.

These ten questions walk you through those three parts for your own task. There is nothing to fill in. Read them slowly and answer them in your head.

QuestionMaps to
1What is the actual decision at the end of this task?The choice
2How often do you do this, and what sets it off?Trigger
3Where do you look before you decide? Name every place.Grounding/Context
4What are you actually comparing across those sources?Grounding/Context
5How do you weigh them? What makes it a yes and not a no?Skill
6Think of the last time this was genuinely hard. What made it hard?Skill
7What did you notice that a less experienced person would have missed?Skill
8If a sharp new starter did this from your notes, where would they get it wrong?Skill
9Who is allowed to make the final call, and what happens if it is wrong?Human gate
10Once you have decided, what do you do, and where does it get recorded?Write-back

Of all that — how much time went on the deciding, and how much on the gathering?

Four reasons to stop and think

  • Judgment that has to stay with a person. Anything where the call itself is the job.
  • Irreversible actions with no review step. If it cannot be undone, it does not go out unchecked.
  • Data that cannot go to a third-party vendor under company policy. Check before, not after.
  • A process that is already broken. Automation makes a process faster, not better. Fix it first.

A red flag usually does not kill the task. It means you automate the drafting and keep the send action human.

06 — Trust and control

Nothing acts unwatched

No message sends, no record updates and no client sees anything without a person approving it first. That is not a concession bolted on to reassure people. It is structural — the human gate is one of the six parts, and a workflow without one is not finished.

AI cannot be accountable. People can.

Which is exactly why the final go/no-go stays with a person. Accountability is not a task you can delegate to software, so the decision it attaches to stays put.

Interactive

Be the gate

Four drafts are queued and ready to send. Three are fine. One should not go out. You have the time it takes to read them — which is the point.

The part nobody mentions

A workflow is not finished when it works. It is finished when someone owns it.

Things move. A field gets renamed, a process changes, a team reorganises, a source moves. The workflow does not announce any of this. It carries on running and quietly produces output that is slightly wrong — and because the output looks exactly like it did last month, nobody notices for a while.

This is the most common way these things fail in practice, and it is nothing like the failure people expect. The fear is that the AI will do something dramatic. The reality is that it keeps doing something reasonable long after the reasonable thing changed.

Two consequences. Every workflow needs a named owner — a person, not a team. And the human gate is not only there to catch bad output, it is there to make sure somebody is still looking.
07 — Where we already are

This is not new to the company. It is new to your desk.

AI inside your own product
Whatever machine learning already ships inside what your company sells — classifiers, ranking, detection, generated content. Mature, running at scale, and entirely internal to the product.
AI in Engineering
Coding assistants, agentic investigations, issue analysis. Usually the highest adoption in any company, and no case left to make. Also internal — tied to engineering systems and workflows.
Everywhere else
Sales, Business Development, Marketing, Client Success, Operations, Finance, HR. Client-facing, judgement-heavy, and almost entirely untouched. This is the gap.

The first two columns are proof that the company can build and run this kind of thing well. Neither of them is connected to anything a non-engineer could pick up, and neither of them touches the work in the third column. That is the whole opportunity.

Two real shapes of workflow. One is about volume, one is about the length of the path.

Before

  1. Read each generated recommendation, one at a time
  2. Look up the person responsible for that account
  3. Draft a message specific to that person and that recommendation
  4. Send it

After

  • Approvals and responses recorded as they come back

Build these

Two you could put into your business today

Neither of these is exotic and neither needs a project. They are the two patterns that turn up in almost every organisation, in almost every team, and you will probably recognise your own version of both.

One
The account narrative

The CRM already holds rich context — emails, meeting notes, recovery plans, health scores, survey responses — alongside whatever sits in the data warehouse. Today somebody has to hunt through all of it, and some of those plans run long.

Instead, a clean narrative on request:

The ask, as it would actually be phrased
I hear there are technical issues with this account — summarise the current risks, owners, blockers and next steps, and highlight who owns the next step on anything technical.

The value is not the summary. It is the search across every source at once, which is the part no person can do quickly. Whoever owns the relationship benefits, and so does anyone above them who needs the picture without the digging.

Two
Data and process hygiene

Most organisations have trained people on the process and still have gaps in the data. Finding those gaps is manual: somebody goes looking, then chases people individually.

The ask, as it would actually be phrased
Show me every active plan in this segment with overdue actions, missing owners, or no update in the last month.

Exceptions surfaced in seconds instead of found by hand. Longer term it could chase people directly, but simply surfacing the gaps is most of the gain.

Note the shape: this one has no client-facing output at all, which makes it one of the safest possible places to start.

One pulls scattered context into a single answer. The other finds the exceptions nobody has time to look for. Between them they cover most of what is worth building first — and both depend on exactly one thing, which is whether the workflow can reach the records, the numbers and the mailbox at the same time. That is the next section.
09 — The thing underneath all of it

Scattered information, brought somewhere it can be reached

Every example above depends on the same thing: the workflow could get to the information it needed. That is the quiet constraint on all of this. Not model quality — reach.

A knowledge layer is simply the answer to "where does the truth live, and can it be read?" Shared across the company rather than rebuilt per team, because the same account, the same customer and the same history show up in half a dozen different people's work.

08 — The honest part

What a before-and-after leaves out

Both of those look clean written down, which is a problem with writing things down.

The outreach workflow — two rounds
The first version did not work. It was too big and too detailed: it carried over extra information that made sense to engineering and made none to the people receiving the message. Too much text, hard to process, confusing format. Two rounds of iteration to fix, and neither of the problems was interesting.
The investigation workflow — twenty-plus
Built first as a script, it went through more than twenty versions and still fought back. Rebuilt with proper context, shared knowledge, defined queries and guard rails already in place, it came together faster and more easily. The model was not the variable. What it could reach was.

That gap between twenty-plus versions and a handful is the most useful number in this document, and it is the clearest evidence for the knowledge layer described in the section above. The same person building the same workflow twice, and the difference was how much the thing already knew before it started.

What you are really doing in those early rounds is discovering the parts of the procedure you thought you had written down and had not — the assumptions so obvious to you that it never occurred to you to state them. Same lesson as the burger, arriving a second time and costing more. Budget for it. A workflow that saves two days a month is worth a week of getting right, but it is a week, not an afternoon.

Measuring it without fooling yourself

The usual measure is time saved, and it is almost useless. It is self-reported, it is never checked, and everyone involved has an incentive for the number to be large.

A better measure is sitting in front of you every time you use one of these: how much of the output do you change before it goes out? On a working workflow that is three or four quick edits per run — small, fast, and material enough to be worth making. Every edit is a place where the instruction did not say what you meant, which makes the number a direct read on the quality of the procedure rather than on the quality of the AI.

Falling
The procedure is improving. Corrections you fed back have made the next run better.
Flat and high
There is a gap you have not identified. You are correcting the same thing repeatedly instead of fixing it once.
Rising
Something underneath has changed and nobody noticed. This is the early warning for the rot described above, and it shows up here first.

One thing this will not tell you is whether the workflow was worth building at all. That is frequency multiplied by the time each run takes, set against the time it took to build and will take to maintain.

10 — At scale

What happens when there are forty of these

Everything above is written for one person and one task, which is the right place to start and the wrong place to stop. The problems change shape once workflows multiply: who writes them, who maintains them when the author moves team, whether two departments have quietly built the same thing twice, and what happens the first time one produces something wrong and reaches a client. None of those are technology questions, and none get easier by being ignored until they arrive.

Central
One team builds and maintains workflows for everyone. Consistent, reviewable, safe — and a queue. It also concentrates the knowledge in the place furthest from the work, which is precisely the wrong place for it.
Embedded
Each department builds its own. Fast, close to the work, immediately relevant. It also produces duplication, uneven quality, and no shared standard for what a good procedure looks like.
Embedded, with a central specialist function
Neither model works alone. The procedures have to be written by the people who run the work, because that is where the knowledge is and no central team can extract it faster than the people holding it can write it down. What belongs in one place is everything around the procedures: the standards, the shared knowledge layer, the review of anything client-facing, and the enablement that makes the first attempt survivable. Skills sit with the people who hold them. Support, standards and reach sit centrally.

Whichever model you land on, decide it deliberately. The default is that it happens by accident, department by department — and by the time anyone looks up there are forty workflows, no register of what exists, and nobody who can say which of them still work.

05 — The four things people say next

Pick the one you are actually thinking

"Is this going to replace my job?"+

The honest answer has two halves and you deserve both.

The first half: the gathering is going away. Not immediately and not everywhere, but the part of your week spent assembling information so that you can make a decision is the part this is genuinely good at, and it is not coming back. Anyone telling you otherwise is managing you rather than informing you.

The second half: the deciding is not going away, and the accountability attached to it cannot go anywhere. Software cannot be answerable for an outcome. It cannot be asked why, it cannot be held to it, and it cannot carry the consequence. All of that stays with a person, which means the decision it attaches to stays with a person too.

What changes is the ratio. If you currently spend most of your week gathering and a little of it deciding, that inverts. Whether that is a better job or a worse one depends largely on which half you got into this work for.

"My task needs judgment."+
It does, and the judgment stays yours. What gets handed over is the gathering that happens before the judgment — the reading, the cross-checking, the hunting through four systems for the one number you needed. You still make the call. You just arrive at it with everything already in front of you.
"Every case is different."+
Different inputs, same procedure. If every case were truly different you could not train anyone to do this job. You do train people. Therefore there is a procedure — it is just living in your head instead of on paper.
"I would have to check the output anyway."+
Yes. That is the design, not a flaw in it. Checking a draft takes minutes. Producing that draft took hours. The whole trade is moving your time from production to review — and review is where your experience is worth the most anyway.
11 — How do I start

Start by thinking differently about Monday

There is no form to fill in. The first step is a change in how you look at your own week.

  1. Look at the processes you run today — the recurring ones, the ones you would describe as admin, the ones you dread.
  2. Against what you have just read, ask which of them fit. Where is the decision? Where is the gathering?
  3. Pick the one that is most obviously mostly-gathering. That is your candidate.
  4. If you want help turning it into something real, ask. That part is not the hard part.

The point of this document was never to sell you a tool. It was to change what you notice. Once you start seeing the gathering as separate from the deciding, you will not be able to stop seeing it — and that is the whole beginning.

The whole argument in one place

Get the right context in front of the right question. Be precise about what problem is being solved and what outcome is needed. Then execute — with one agent or several working together, which matters far less than people think.

The hard part was never the execution. It is that most of us have never broken the decision down or written it out — which is why the work cannot be handed over, why it cannot be taught quickly, and why it leaves the building when we do.
